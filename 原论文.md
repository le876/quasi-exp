# Abstract

This paper explores motion control of cable-driven continuum robots based on quasi-static dynamics for low-speed scenarios, particularly suitable for the inspection and clearance of structures within the confined and complex internal spaces of aircraft components. In such applications, the motion control of continuum robots is characterized by significant self-weight, high structural coupling, and large driving forces. The study analyzes the impact of significant self-weight and complex structures on control accuracy, highlighting the limitations of traditional models and data-driven methods. A combined approach is proposed, integrating a piecewise variable curvature kinematic model with quasi-static dynamic equations and machine learning. A virtual prototype model was constructed, and simulation data were generated using Monte Carlo and particle swarm optimization to train a multilayer neural network for path-following control. Simulation results show significant improvements in control accuracy, reduced robot damage, and enhanced real-time performance. The maximum end error is  $9.37\mathrm{mm}$ , and the average inference time is  $0.72\mathrm{ms}$ . This method offers balanced optimization in motion control accuracy, robot damage reduction, and real-time performance, outperforming traditional methods. It provides a new solution for continuum robots with significant self-weight, high structural coupling, and large driving forces, offering valuable insights for related research and applications.

Keywords: Continuum robots; Quasi-static dynamic; Machine learning; Piecewise variable curvature; Path following;

# 1. Introduction

Cable-driven continuum robots, due to their flexibility and high degrees of freedom, have shown significant advantages in medical, industrial applications, disaster rescue, and nuclear industry maintenance  $^{1-4}$ . These robots are also crucial for inspecting and maintaining the complex and narrow internal structures of aircraft, significantly enhancing operational safety and efficiency in aviation maintenance and repair tasks  $^{5-12}$ . They can perform precise operations and remote maintenance in narrow,

complex environments, thereby improving safety and efficiency. This paper aims to address the path-following problem of cable-driven continuum robots operating under significant self-weight, high structural coupling, and large driving forces  $^{13-18}$ . The significant self-weight results from the requirement for these robots to carry end-effectors, such as grippers or inspection cameras, which necessitate sufficient load-bearing capacity, leading to increased self-weight, high structural coupling, and large driving forces. This is particularly relevant for inspecting and clearing structures within the confined, complex internal spaces

of aircraft components.

The focus of this study is achieving precise path-following under conditions of significant self-weight, complex structural dynamics, and large driving forces, with the goal of improving motion control accuracy, enhancing real-time performance, and minimizing motion-related damage. However, this problem presents several challenges:

Significant Self-weight and Structural Complexity: Large continuum robots are characterized by substantial weight and tightly coupled drive cables, necessitating considerable driving force, which complicates control and often reduces motion precision. Continuum robot segments are typically modeled as constant curvature (CC) arcs to derive the kinematic model  $^{19,20}$ . This assumption is effective for short segments with minimal deviation or low precision requirements. However, for the proposed robot, there are significant discrepancies between the actual shapes and the CC model, leading to substantial errors  $^{21}$ . In multi-segment robots, distal segment cables pass through proximal segments, resulting in additional torques and forces on proximal segments, which affects stability  $^{22}$ . Additionally, the total cable length required for distal segment control depends on both proximal and distal segments  $^{23}$ .

Limitations of Analytical Model Methods: Traditional analytical models for continuum robot kinematics and dynamics struggle with high nonlinearity and multi-segment coupling, making precise control difficult  $^{24-26}$ . Solving these models often requires numerical optimization, which limits real-time performance  $^{21,27}$ . For instance, Yuan et al.  $^{27}$  showed that a numerical optimization algorithm on a 3.5 GHz CPU PC took an average of 262 milliseconds per sample for a robot with 3 segments and 9 drive cables, underscoring its real-time limitations.

Limitations of Data-Driven Methods: Pure black-box or fully data-driven machine learning approaches require extensive real robot data  $^{28,29}$ , which can lead to damage, particularly to the drive cables and friction-exposed components. This wear reduces data validity and convergence during training, ultimately decreasing motion control accuracy.

To address these challenges, this study proposes an integrated approach. For significant self-weight and structural complexity, we combine a kinematic model based on the piecewise variable curvature assumption with quasi-static dynamic equations, providing a precise foundation for posture reconstruction and path-following control. To overcome the limitations of analytical models, we integrate them with learning algorithms, improving both real-time performance and control accuracy.

As shown in Fig. 1, we begin by studying the kinematics of the continuum robot using the piecewise variable curvature model, which provides accurate kinematic modeling based on the bending angles of the

robot's disks. We then derive recursive quasi-static dynamic equations that account for the robot's structural complexity and driving forces. This comprehensive model integrates kinematic properties with quasi-static dynamics constraints. Using a virtual prototype, we generate a training set for machine learning by simulating the robot's motion and mechanical behavior in various scenarios. This enables neural network training with simulation data to learn the inverse dynamics model, optimizing path-following control. By inputting task space targets and generating intermediate outputs in the drive space, we achieve precise path-following control for the actual robot.

# 3. Quasi-static Dynamics of Continuum Robots

# 3.1. Applicability Analysis of Quasi-static Dynamics for Continuum Robots

In this section, we present the dynamics modeling of the continuum robot and justify the reduction to a quasi-static dynamics model under low-speed conditions. We start by defining the full dynamics equations, including inertial and velocity-dependent terms, and then perform an applicability analysis to demonstrate that these terms can be neglected due to the robot's operating conditions. This allows us to focus on the dynamics of gravitational forces, cable tensions, bending moments, and frictional effects, which are critical for accurate modeling and control in our application. This section discusses the reduction of continuum dynamics modeling to a quasi-static

dynamics model under low-speed conditions, focusing on the applicability analysis. Specifically, it examines the reasonableness of simplifying the dynamics problem to a quasi static dynamics problem by neglecting the robot's dynamic velocity and acceleration terms.

In robotics, dynamics refers to the study of how forces and torques affect the motion of robots, accounting for inertial effects due to acceleration, velocity-dependent forces like Coriolis and centrifugal forces, gravitational forces, and external forces. Statics deals with analyzing robots in static equilibrium, where all forces and torques balance out, and there is no acceleration. Quasi-static dynamics bridges these concepts by considering scenarios where inertial and velocity-dependent effects are negligible due to low-speed operation, but dynamic principles still govern the force and moment interactions within the robot.

In fact, without considering the joint linear damping force, the dynamics equation of any robot can be written in the following standard form<sup>32</sup>:

$$
\boldsymbol {M} (\boldsymbol {q}) \ddot {\boldsymbol {q}} + \boldsymbol {V} (\boldsymbol {q}, \dot {\boldsymbol {q}}) \dot {\boldsymbol {q}} + \boldsymbol {G} (\boldsymbol {q}) + \boldsymbol {J} ^ {\mathrm {T}} \boldsymbol {F} _ {\mathrm {e}} = \boldsymbol {\tau} \tag {9}
$$

In the equation,  $\pmb{q}$  is an  $n \times 1$  joint displacement vector;  $M(\pmb{q})$  is an  $n \times n$  generalized mass matrix in joint space, which is related to the robot's configuration;  $V(\pmb{q}, \dot{\pmb{q}})$  is an  $n \times n$  matrix of Coriolis and centrifugal force coupling coefficients in joint space, related to the robot's configuration and velocity;  $G(\pmb{q})$  is an  $n \times 1$  joint force vector resisting gravity, related to the robot's configuration;  $F_{\mathrm{e}}$  is a  $6 \times 1$  contact force vector exerted by the robot on the environment, defined in the operational space;  $J$  is the  $6 \times n$  velocity Jacobian matrix of the robot; and  $\pmb{\tau}$  is an  $n \times 1$  joint force/torque vector.

In this paper, we adopt the quasi-static dynamics model of the robot to replace the full dynamics model. This is done by setting  $\ddot{\pmb{q}} = \mathbf{0}$  and  $\dot{\pmb{q}} = \mathbf{0}$  in Equation (9). This simplification enables the use of the forward dynamics model to implement the virtual prototype model described in Section 5, and the inverse dynamics model for the subsequent motion control of the continuum robot. This section provides a detailed discussion and analysis of the rationale for using the quasi-static dynamics model instead of the full dynamics model. In summary, we aim to demonstrate that in practical robot control, the terms  $M(\pmb{q})\ddot{\pmb{q}}$  (acceleration term) and  $V(\pmb{q},\dot{\pmb{q}})\dot{\pmb{q}}$  (velocity term) can be neglected relative to the term  $G(\pmb{q})$  (gravity term). Here, the external force  $\pmb{F}_{\mathrm{e}}$  is considered to be zero.

To simplify the theoretical analysis in this section, we will perform the relevant analysis using a single-section continuum, which is a special case with  $k = 1$  as described in Section 2. We will discuss the order of magnitude relationships between the acceleration term, velocity term, and gravity term for a single-section continuum arm in its initial state, where  $\theta_{i} = 0$ , to demonstrate that the acceleration and velocity terms

can be neglected.

# 3.1.1. Acceleration Term Analysis

Let the odd-numbered terms of the single-section continuum robot be  $\theta_{i} = 0$ ,  $\dot{\theta}_{i} = 0$ , and  $\ddot{\theta}_{i} = 0$ , meaning that the driving cables controlling the up-and-down pitch degree of freedom of the single-section continuum robotic arm are stationary in the initial state. Let the even-numbered terms be  $\theta_{i} = 0$ ,  $\dot{\theta}_{i} = 0$ , and  $\ddot{\theta}_{i} = \epsilon$ , where  $\epsilon$  is the maximum joint angular acceleration produced during actual control, meaning that the driving cables controlling the left-and-right swing degree of freedom of the single-section continuum robotic arm act with maximum acceleration from an initial velocity of zero. In actual control, the maximum speed of the driving cables used in this paper is  $v_{\mathrm{rope}} = 10~\mathrm{mm / min} = \frac{1}{6}\mathrm{mm / s}$ , and the fastest acceleration time is 1 second, giving a maximum driving cable acceleration of  $a_{\mathrm{rope}} = \frac{1}{6} mm / s^2$ .

Let the total length of the single-section continuum robot be  $L$ , and the distance from the driving holes of the cables controlling the left-and-right swing degree of freedom to the central axis of the continuum be  $d_{\mathrm{rope}}$ . Assuming the single-section continuum robotic arm approximates a constant curvature bending motion, the acceleration  $a_{\mathrm{end}}$  of the end disk in Cartesian coordinates can be expressed as:

$$
a _ {\text {e n d}} = 5 \epsilon L \tag {10}
$$

When the even-numbered terms have  $\ddot{\theta}_t = \epsilon$ , the actuation acceleration of the driving cable  $a_{\mathrm{rope}}$  is:

$$
a _ {\text {r o p e}} = 5 \epsilon d _ {\text {r o p e}} \tag {11}
$$

Therefore, the acceleration  $a_{end}$  of the end disk in Cartesian coordinates can be expressed using the actuation acceleration  $a_{\mathrm{rope}}$  of the driving cable as follows:

$$
a _ {\text {e n d}} = \frac {L}{d _ {\text {r o p e}}} a _ {\text {r o p e}} \tag {12}
$$

After eliminating terms such as the disk mass, the ratio of the acceleration term to the gravity term in the dynamics equation of the end disk is given by:

$$
\frac {\boldsymbol {M} (\boldsymbol {q}) \ddot {\boldsymbol {q}}}{\boldsymbol {G} (\boldsymbol {q})} = \frac {a _ {\text {e n d}}}{\mathrm {g}} = \frac {\boldsymbol {L} \cdot \boldsymbol {a} _ {\text {r o p e}}}{d _ {\text {r o p e}} \cdot \mathrm {g}} \tag {13}
$$

Substituting  $a_{\mathrm{rope}} = \frac{1}{6} \, \mathrm{mm/s}^2$ ,  $g = 9.8 \, \mathrm{m/s}^2$ ,  $L = 450 \, \mathrm{mm}$ , and  $d_{\mathrm{rope}} = 20 \, \mathrm{mm}$ , the ratio of the acceleration term to the gravity term for the end disk is:  $\frac{M(q) \tilde{q}}{G(q)} = 3.826 \times 10^{-4}$ . The order of magnitude difference between the two terms is significant, thus justifying the omission of the acceleration term in the continuum robot dynamics. The parameters used here are intrinsic to the robot under study.

In this comparison, we focus on the ratio of the acceleration term  $M(q)\ddot{q}$  to the gravity term  $G(q)$  to prove that the acceleration term is negligible. This

allows us to ignore it without affecting the accuracy of deriving the driving torque  $\pmb{\tau}$  from the gravity term.

# 3.1.2. Velocity Term Analysis

Similar to the acceleration term analysis, we discuss the velocity term analysis at specific points. Let the odd-numbered terms of the single-section continuum robot be  $\theta_{i} = 0$ ,  $\dot{\theta}_{i} = 0$ , and  $\ddot{\theta}_{i} = 0$ , meaning that the driving cables controlling the up-and-down pitch degree of freedom of the single-section continuum robotic arm are stationary in the initial state. Let the even-numbered terms be  $\theta_{i} = 0$ ,  $\dot{\theta}_{i} = \delta$ , and  $\ddot{\theta}_{i} = 0$ , where  $\delta$  is the maximum joint angular velocity produced during actual control. This means that the driving cables controlling the left-and-right swing degree of freedom of the single-section continuum robotic arm act with maximum velocity in the initial state. Similar to the acceleration term analysis, in actual control, the maximum speed of the driving cables used in this paper is  $v_{\mathrm{rope}} = 10~\mathrm{mm / min} = \frac{1}{6}\mathrm{mm / s}$ .

When the even-numbered terms have  $\dot{\theta}_i = \delta$ , assuming the single-section continuum robotic arm approximates a constant curvature bending motion, the actuation speed  $v_{\mathrm{rope}}$  of the driving cable is given by:

$$
v _ {\text {r o p e}} = 5 \delta d _ {\text {r o p e}} \tag {14}
$$

Given that the distance from the center of mass of the end disk to the origin  $O_{10}$  of the local coordinate system  $\{10\}$  is  $l_{\mathrm{Gmas}}$ , the angular velocity  $\omega_{\mathrm{end}}$  of the end disk relative to the base coordinate system  $\{0\}$  is:

$$
\omega_ {\text {e n d}} = 5 \delta \tag {15}
$$

After eliminating terms such as the disk mass, the ratio of the velocity term to the gravity term in the dynamics equation of the end disk is:

$$
\begin{array}{l} \frac {\boldsymbol {V} (\boldsymbol {q} , \dot {\boldsymbol {q}}) \dot {\boldsymbol {q}}}{\boldsymbol {G} (\boldsymbol {q})} = \frac {\omega_ {\text {e n d}} ^ {2} \cdot m _ {\text {e n d}} \cdot l _ {\text {m a s s G}} ^ {2}}{m _ {\text {e n d}} \cdot \mathrm {g} \cdot l _ {\text {m a s s G}}} \\ = \frac {v _ {\mathrm {r o p e}} ^ {2} \cdot l _ {\mathrm {G m a s s}}}{d _ {\mathrm {r o p e}} ^ {2} \cdot \mathrm {g}} \\ \end{array}
$$

(16)

Substituting  $v_{\mathrm{rope}} = \frac{1}{6} \mathrm{mm/s}$ ,  $\mathbf{g} = 9.8 \mathrm{m/s}^2$ ,  $l_{\mathrm{Gmass}} = 22.5 \mathrm{mm}$ , and  $d_{\mathrm{rope}} = 20 \mathrm{mm}$ , the ratio of the velocity term to the gravity term for the end disk is:  $\frac{V(q, q) q}{G(q)} = 1.594 \times 10^{-7}$ . The order of magnitude difference between the two terms is significant, thus justifying the omission of the velocity term in the continuum robot dynamics.

Similarly, we compare the velocity term  $V(\mathbf{q}, \dot{\mathbf{q}}) \dot{\mathbf{q}}$  directly to the gravity term  $G(\mathbf{q})$ . By demonstrating that this ratio is very small, we justify neglecting the velocity term, ensuring that this simplification does not impact the accuracy of calculating the driving torque.

# 3.2.1. Gravitational Term

Due to the highly slender design of the continuum robot in this paper (diameter-to-length ratio  $< 0.05$ ), the gravitational effect of the disks cannot be ignored. Gravity acts along the negative direction of the  $y_0$  axis in the base coordinate system  $\{0\}$ . Thus, for the  $i_{th}$  disk (including the weight of other components in this section), the gravitational vector in the base coordinate system  $\{0\}$  can be written as:

$$
^ 0 \mathbf {G} _ {i} = \left[ \begin{array}{l l l l} 0 & - m _ {i} \cdot \mathrm {g} & 0 & 1 \end{array} \right] ^ {\mathrm {T}} \tag {17}
$$

In the local coordinate system  $\{i\}$ , it can be written as:

$$
^ {i} \mathbf {G} _ {i} = \left(^ {0} _ {i} T\right) ^ {- 1} \cdot {} ^ {0} \mathbf {G} _ {i} \tag {18}
$$

The gravitational action point of the  $i_{th}$  disk is at the center of mass  $O_{G_i}$ , with the direction opposite to the positive direction of the  $y_0$  axis in the base coordinate system  $\{0\}$ . In the local coordinate system  $\{i\}$ , taking the moment of the gravitational force about the point  $O_i$ :

$$
{ } ^ { i } \mathbf { M } _ { G _ { i } } = \left( ^ { i } O _ { G _ { i } } - ^ { i } O _ { i } \right) \times { } ^ { i } \mathbf { G } _ { i } \tag {19}
$$

Similarly, extending this to all disks, the moment of the gravitational force about  $O_{i}$  for each disk can be expressed as:

$$
{ } ^ { i } \mathbf { M } _ { G _ { p } } = \left( ^ { i } O _ { G _ { p } } - ^ { i } O _ { i } \right) \times { } ^ { i } \mathbf { G } _ { p }  ( 2 0 )
$$

In this expression,  $i \leq p \leq kD$ .

In the local coordinate system  $\{i\}$ , it can be written as:

$$
^ {i} O _ {G _ {p}} = \mathbf {\Pi} _ {p} ^ {i} \mathbf {T} \cdot {} ^ {p} O _ {G _ {p}} \tag {21}
$$

$$
^ {i} \mathbf {G} _ {p} = \left(_ {i} ^ {0} \mathbf {T}\right) ^ {- 1} \cdot {} ^ {0} \mathbf {G} _ {p} \tag {22}
$$

# 3.2.2. Driving Cable Tension Term

As shown in Fig. 5, taking the driving tension of the  $j_{th}$  cable as an example, since the segment from the  $i_{th}$  disk to the last disk is analyzed as a whole, the force exerted by the cable on the segment equals the tension  $F_{CT_{i,j}}$ . The tension  $F_{CT_{i,j}}$  represents the tension in the  $j_{th}$  cable on the segment between the  $i_{th}$  disk and the  $i - 1_{th}$  disk, with the direction from hole  $h_{i,j}$  to hole  $h_{i - 1,j}'$ . Here, the superscript ' denotes the distal (far from the robot's base) opening of the hole. It is assumed that the driving cables can only transmit tensile forces (they can be pulled but not compressed). Therefore, the cable tensions  $F_{CT_{i,j}}$  are always non-negative, and any potential compressive forces are not possible in the cables. This unidirectional force transmission is integral to our mechanical model and control strategy.

The moment of the force  $F_{CT_{i,j}}$  about point  $O_i$  in the local coordinate system  $\{i\}$  is given by:

$$
{ } ^ { i } \mathbf { M } _ { F _ { C T _ { i , j } } } = ( { } ^ { i } h _ { i , j } - { } ^ { i } O _ { i } ) \times { } ^ { i } \mathbf { F } _ { C T _ { i , j } } \tag {23}
$$

The cable tension  ${}^i\mathbf{F}_{CT_{i,j}}$  represents the vector expression in the local coordinate system  $\{i\}$ . The magnitude of the tension is  $F_{CT_{i,j}}$ , and its unit direction is given by:

$$
\frac {\mathbf {i} h _ {i - 1 , j} ^ {\prime} - \mathbf {i} h _ {i , j}}{\| \mathbf {i} h _ {i - 1 , j} ^ {\prime} - \mathbf {i} h _ {i , j} \|} \tag {24}
$$

In this unit vector,  ${}^i h_{i,j}$  represents the homogeneous coordinates of hole  $h_{i,j}$  in the local coordinate system  $\{i\}$ , and  ${}^i h_{i-1,j}'$  represents the homogeneous coordinates of hole  $h_{i-1,j}'$  in the local coordinate system  $\{i\}$ . Thus, we have:

$$
{ } ^ { i } h _ { i - 1 , j } ^ { \prime } = ( \mathbf { \Gamma } _ { i } ^ { i - 1 } \mathbf { T } ) ^ { - 1 } \cdot \mathbf { \Gamma } ^ { i - 1 } h _ { i - 1 , j } ^ { \prime } \tag {25}
$$

The homogeneous transformation matrix  $\mathbf{\Pi}_i^{i - 1}\mathbf{T}$  can be obtained in the kinematic derivation section. Similarly, the moment of  $F_{CT_{i,j}}$  about the  $z_{i}$ -axis is given by:

$$
{ } ^ { i } \mathbf { M } _ { F _ { C T _ { i , j } } , z _ { i } } = { } ^ { i } \mathbf { M } _ { F _ { C T _ { i , j } } } \cdot { } ^ { i } \mathbf { e } _ { z _ { i } } \tag {26}
$$

Similarly, the moments and the components of the moments along the  $z_{i}$ -axis for the other driving cables can also be determined.

3.2.3. Bending Moment Term of the Superelastic Nitinol Rod

Assuming that the axial deformation of the elastic body is neglected, and due to structural constraints, there is no torsional deformation of the elastic body, only bending deformation is considered. Each segment of the elastic body is assumed to be a Kirchhoff-Love beam, and each small segment is considered to be a circular arc. The homogeneous vector of the bending moment of the elastic body between the  $i - 1_{th}$  disk and the  $i_{th}$  disk is given by:

$$
{ } ^ { i } \mathbf { M } _ { \mathrm { b n d } _ { i } } = \left[ \begin{array} { c c c } 0 & 0 & \frac { \theta _ { i } } { l _ { i } } \cdot E _ { i } \cdot I _ { z _ { i } } \\ & & 1 \end{array} \right] \tag {27}
$$

Here,  $\frac{\theta_i}{l_i}$  is the curvature of the elastic body between the  $i - 1_{th}$  disk and the  $i_{th}$  disk,  $E_{i}$  is the elastic modulus of the superelastic Nitinol rod, and  $I_{z_i}$  is its area moment of inertia about the  $z_{i}$ -axis. It is important to note that  ${}^i\mathbf{M}_{\mathrm{bnd}_i}$  represents the bending moment of the elastic body in the local coordinate system  $\{i\}$ .

3.2.4. Support Force Term from Shaft  $s_i$  on the Disks

The support force exerted by the shaft  $s_i$  on the  $i_{th}$  disk can be represented as  $F_{s_i}$ . This support force can be decomposed into an axial force along the  $x_i$ -axis,  $F_{s_i,\text{axial}}$ , and a radial force along the  $y_i$ -axis,  $F_{s_i,\text{radial}}$ .

$$
^ {i} \mathbf {F} _ {s _ {i}} = F _ {s _ {i}, \text {a x i a l}} \cdot {} ^ {i} \mathbf {e} _ {x _ {i}} + F _ {s _ {i}, \text {r a d i c a l}} \cdot {} ^ {i} \mathbf {e} _ {y _ {i}} \tag {28}
$$

Here,  ${}^i\mathbf{e}_{x_i}$  and  ${}^i\mathbf{e}_{y_i}$  are the unit vectors along the  $x_{i}$  -axis and  $y_{i}$  -axis, respectively, in the local coordinate system  $\{i\}$ .

The moment of the support force about point  $O_{i}$  is given by:

$$
{ } ^ { i } \mathbf { M } _ { F _ { s _ { i } } } = \mathbf { 0 } \times { } ^ { i } \mathbf { F } _ { s _ { i } } = \mathbf { 0 } \tag {29}
$$

# 3.2.5. External Force and Moment Term

In this paper, the external force acting on the  $p_{th}$  disk is denoted as  ${}^0\mathbf{F}_{exp}$ , where the superscript 0 indicates that it is represented in the base coordinate system  $\{0\}$ . The external moment acting on the  $p_{th}$  disk is denoted as  ${}^0\mathbf{M}_{exp}$ , with the superscript 0 indicating representation in the base coordinate system  $\{0\}$ . Here,  $0 < p \leq kD$ .

When  $p = kD$ ,  ${}^0\mathbf{F}_{ex_{kD}}$  and  ${}^0\mathbf{M}_{ex_{kD}}$  are the external concentrated force and external concentrated moment acting on the end disk of the continuum robot. When  $p < kD$ ,  ${}^0\mathbf{F}_{ex_{kD}}$  and  ${}^0\mathbf{M}_{ex_{kD}}$  are the contact force and moment acting on the middle section of the robot. It is particularly noted that the most common external force and moment often act on the end section of the continuum robot.

These forces and moments, when mapped from the base coordinate system  $\{0\}$  to the local coordinate system  $\{i\}$ , are given by:

$$
{ } ^ { i } \mathbf { F } _ { e x _ { p } } = ( \mathbf { \Sigma } _ { i } ^ { 0 } \mathbf { T } ) ^ { - 1 } \cdot \mathbf { \Sigma } ^ { 0 } \mathbf { F } _ { e x _ { p } } \tag {30}
$$

$$
{ } ^ { i } \mathbf { M } _ { e x p } = ( \mathbf { \Gamma } _ { i } ^ { 0 } \mathbf { T } ) ^ { - 1 } \cdot \mathbf { \Gamma } ^ { 0 } \mathbf { M } _ { e x p } \tag {31}
$$

For  $F_{ex_p}$ , the moment about point  $O_i$  is given by:

$$
{ } ^ { i } \mathbf { M } _ { F _ { e x p } } = ( \mathbf { \nabla } ^ { i } O _ { F _ { e x p } } - \mathbf { \nabla } ^ { i } O _ { i } ) \times \mathbf { \nabla } ^ { i } \mathbf { F } _ { e x p }  ( 3 2 )
$$

Here,  $O_{F_{exp}}$  is the point of application of  $F_{exp}$  on the  $p_{th}$  disk, also represented in the local coordinate system  $\{i\}$ :

$$
{ } ^ { i } O _ { F _ { e x p } } = { } _ { p } ^ { i } \mathbf { T } \cdot { } ^ { p } O _ { e x p } ( 3 3 )
$$

3.2.6. Frictional Torque Term from Shaft  $s_i$  on the Segment from  $Disk_{i}$  to  $Disk_{kD}$

For the segment from  $Disk_{i}$  to  $Disk_{kD}$ , the force equilibrium equation is given by:

$$
\sum_ {p = i} ^ {k D} \left(^ {i} \mathbf {G} _ {p} + ^ {i} \mathbf {F} _ {e x p}\right) + \sum_ {j} ^ {i} \mathbf {F} _ {C T _ {i, j}} + ^ {i} \mathbf {F} _ {s _ {i}} = 0 \tag {34}
$$

From Equation (34), we can determine the support force  ${}^i\mathbf{F}_{s_i}$  exerted by the shaft  $s_i$ . Assuming the coefficient of friction between the shaft and the disk is  $\mu$  (assuming that the kinetic friction coefficient is approximately equal to the static friction coefficient), and the radius of the shaft is  $r$  (the shaft radius used in the robot is  $4\mathrm{mm}$ ). The magnitude of the frictional torque  ${}^i\mathbf{M}_{f_{s_i}}$  generated by the shaft  $s_i$  is given by:

$$
M _ {f _ {s _ {i}}} = \mu F _ {s _ {i}} r \tag {35}
$$

The direction of the frictional torque  ${}^i\mathbf{M}_{f_{s_i}}$  depends on the historical state between  $Disk_{i}$  and  $Disk_{i - 1}$ . When  $Disk_{i}$  tends to rotate counterclockwise about the  $z_{i}$ -axis in the local coordinate system  $\{i\}$ , the direction of the frictional torque  ${}^i\mathbf{M}_{f_{s_i}}$  is the same as the positive direction of the  $z_{i}$ -axis. Conversely, when  $Disk_{i}$  tends to rotate clockwise about the  $z_{i}$ -axis in the local coordinate system  $\{i\}$ , the direction of the frictional torque  ${}^i\mathbf{M}_{f_{s_i}}$  is opposite to the positive direction of the  $z_{i}$ -axis.

It can be noted that when  $k = 1$ , the quasi-static dynamics of the multi-section continuum degenerate into the quasi-static dynamics of a single-section continuum.

$F_{CT_{i,j}}$  represents the tension in the  $j_{th}$  driving cable between  $\text{Disk}_i$  and  $\text{Disk}_{i-1}$ , with direction from hole  $h_{i,j}$  to hole  $h_{i-1,j}'$ .

Assuming the point friction coefficient of the cable is  $\mu_{c}$  and neglecting the difference between the static and kinetic friction coefficients, we have:

$$

Thus, we have:

$$
F _ {C T _ {i + 1, j}} ^ {\prime} = F _ {C T _ {i, j}} \frac {\cos \left(\frac {\theta_ {i}}{2}\right) + \mu_ {c} \sin \left(\frac {\theta_ {i}}{2}\right)}{\cos \left(\frac {\theta_ {i + 1}}{2}\right) - \mu_ {c} \sin \left(\frac {\theta_ {i + 1}}{2}\right)} \tag {38}

Case 2: When the cable moves from  $Disk_{i}$  to  $Disk_{i-1}$ , the direction of the frictional force exerted by the disk on the cable is from hole  $h_{i,j}$  to hole  $h_{i,j}'$ . Similarly, we have:

F _ {C T _ {i + 1, j}} ^ {\prime} \cos \left(\frac {\theta_ {i + 1}}{2}\right) = F _ {C I T _ {i, j}} - F _ {C T _ {i + 1, j}} ^ {\prime} \mu_ {c} \sin \left(\frac {\theta_ {i + 1}}{2}\right) \tag {39}
$$

$$
F _ {C T _ {i, j}} \cos \left(\frac {\theta_ {i}}{2}\right) = F _ {C I T _ {i, j}} + F _ {C T _ {i + 1, j}} ^ {\prime} \mu \sin \left(\frac {\theta_ {i}}{2}\right) \tag {40}

Similarly, we also have:

# 3.4. Solution of Kinematics and Quasi-static Dynamics Equations

Due to the inherently slow movement speed of the continuum robot (with the maximum driving cable speed less than  $10\mathrm{mm / min}$ ), the robot can be considered to be in a state of static torque and force equilibrium. This is referred to as quasi-static in this

paper.

That is, for the segment from the  $i_{th}$  disk to the last disk at the robot's end, there is moment equilibrium about the point  $^i O_i$ . This means that the gravitational moment, external moment, cable tension moment, Nitinol rod bending moment, support moment, and shaft frictional moment achieve moment equilibrium.

$$
\sum_ {p = i} ^ {k D} \left(^ {i} \mathbf {M} _ {G _ {p}} + ^ {i} \mathbf {M} _ {e x _ {p}} + ^ {i} \mathbf {M} _ {F e x _ {p}}\right) + \sum_ {j} ^ {i} \mathbf {M} _ {F C T _ {L j}} + ^ {i} \mathbf {M} _ {b n d _ {i}} + ^ {i} \mathbf {M} _ {F s _ {i}} + ^ {i} \mathbf {M} _ {F s _ {i}} = 0 \tag {43}
$$

The driving cable number  $j$  takes different values in different sections, as shown in Fig. 3(b):

$$
j \in \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 1 0, 1 1, 1 2 \}, & \text {i f} 1 \leq i \leq D \\ \{1, 2, 3, 4, 9, 1 0, 1 1, 1 2 \}, & \text {i f} D + 1 \leq i \leq 2 D \\ \{1, 2, 1 1, 1 2 \}, & \text {i f} 2 D + 1 \leq i \leq 3 D \end{array} \right. \tag {44}
$$

The support force moment  ${}^i\mathbf{M}_{F_{s_i}}$  is given by:

$$
{ } ^ { i } \mathbf { M } _ { F _ { S _ { i } } } = 0 \tag {45}
$$

Taking the  $z_{i}$ -axis component of the moment on the left side of Equation (43), we have:

$$
\left(\sum_ {p = i} ^ {k D} \left(^ {i} \mathbf {M} _ {c _ {p}} + ^ {i} \mathbf {M} _ {e x _ {p}} + ^ {i} \mathbf {M} _ {F e x _ {p}}\right) + \sum_ {j} ^ {i} \mathbf {M} _ {F C T _ {i, j}} + ^ {i} \mathbf {M} _ {b n d _ {i}} + ^ {i} \mathbf {M} _ {f _ {i}}\right) \cdot {} ^ {i} \mathbf {e} _ {z _ {i}} = 0 \tag {46}
$$

By assigning values from  $kD$  to 1 in parameter  $i$  cycles, we obtain  $kD$  moment equilibrium equations. Since there are  $kD$  unknowns  $\theta_{i}$ , solving these  $kD$  moment equilibrium equations simultaneously allows us to determine all the unknowns  $\theta_{i}$ .

In the system of equations, the given parameters include all external forces and moments  $F_{exp}$ ,  $M_{F_{exp}}$ ; all gravitational terms  $G_{p}$ ; the driving cable tensions at the base  $F_{CTj,o}$ ; the elastic modulus  $E_{i}$  and the area moment of inertia  $I_{z_i}$  for each segment's elastic body; the friction coefficient  $\mu$  between the shaft and the disk; and the friction coefficient  $\mu_{c}$  between the driving cable and the disk.

When external forces are not considered:

$$
F _ {e x _ {p}} = 0 \tag {47}
$$

$$
\boldsymbol {M} _ {F _ {e x p}} = 0 \tag {48}
$$

In Equation (47) and (48),  $F_{\text{exp}}$  and  $M_{F_{\text{exp}}}$  represent external forces and torques due to environmental interactions, which are set to zero under the assumption that the robot is not in contact with any external objects.

The gravitational terms can be obtained by sequentially measuring the mass of each disk and related components. The driving cable tensions at the base can be obtained using tension sensors. The elastic modulus of the superelastic Nitinol rod can be determined through sample testing. The friction coefficient  $\mu$  between the shaft and the disk, as well as the friction coefficient  $\mu_{c}$  between the driving cable and the disk, can both be obtained through experimental testing.

# 4. Neural Network Training Based on Simulation Data

As shown in Fig. 7, the virtual prototype model

established in this paper aligns the coordinate system with the base coordinate system  $\{0\}$  of the continuum robotic arm. The positive directions of the X, Y, and Z axes in the model coincide with the positive directions of the  $x_0$ ,  $y_0$ , and  $z_0$  axes in the base coordinate system. The origin of the model's coordinate system coincides with the origin  $O_0$  in the base coordinate system.

Based on the established virtual prototype model, this paper collects simulation data to facilitate subsequent neural network training. The input of the simulation data is the homogeneous Cartesian coordinates of the center point of the end disk in the base coordinate system  $\{0\}$ , denoted as  $^0 p_{target} = [x,y,z,1]^T$ . The output of the simulation data consists of the required 12 sets of driving cable tensions  $Tension_{j}$  and 30 joint angles  $\theta_{i}$ . A maximum driving force constraint of  $2000\mathrm{N}$  is applied in the simulations, reflecting the tensile strength limit of the steel cables used for actuation. This constraint ensures that the generated cable tensions remain within safe and realistic operational bounds, reducing the risk of potential damage to the robot.

The simulation data collection process is shown in Fig. 8. To ensure the continuum robotic arm moves as smoothly as possible, each segment should approximate a circular arc with uniform curvature. This means the variance among the odd joint angles and the variance among the even joint angles should both approach zero. Therefore, multiple sets of parameter combinations determining the degrees of freedom of the continuum are generated using the Monte Carlo method:

$$
\boldsymbol {\beta} = \left(\beta_ {1}, \beta_ {2}, \beta_ {3}, \beta_ {4}, \beta_ {5}, \beta_ {6}\right) \tag {49}
$$

Fig. 8 Simulation data collection process diagram.


For the 30 joint angles of the continuum robotic arm, we have:

$$
\theta_ {i} = \left\{ \begin{array}{l l} \beta_ {1}, & i \in \{1, 3, 5, 7, 9 \} \\ \beta_ {2}, & i \in \{2, 4, 6, 8, 1 0 \} \\ \beta_ {3}, & i \in \{1 1, 1 3, 1 5, 1 7, 1 9 \} \\ \beta_ {4}, & i \in \{1 2, 1 4, 1 6, 1 8, 2 0 \} \\ \beta_ {5}, & i \in \{2 1, 2 3, 2 5, 2 7, 2 9 \} \\ \beta_ {6}, & i \in \{2 2, 2 4, 2 6, 2 8, 3 0 \} \end{array} \right. \tag {50}
$$

Using the kinematic equations (4) of the multisection continuum, the equation relating the Cartesian coordinates of the center point of the end disk  ${}^{0}p_{target}$  and the 30 joint angles  $\theta_{i}$  is given by:

$$
{ } ^ { 0 } p _ { t a r g e t } = \begin{array} { c } 0 \\ 3 0 \end{array} T \cdot \begin{array} { c } 3 0 \\ 0 \end{array} p _ { e n d } \tag {51}
$$

Here,  $\mathbf{\Omega}_{30}^{0}\mathbf{T}$  is the homogeneous transformation matrix of the local coordinate system  $\{30\}$  of the end disk in the base coordinate system, and  ${}^{30}p_{end}$  is the homogeneous coordinate representation of the center point of the end disk in the local coordinate system  $\{30\}$ .

Using the quasi-static dynamics moment equilibrium equations (46), the 30 sets of quasi-static dynamics moment equilibrium equations at the rotary joints are given by:

$$
f \left(\theta_ {i}, T e n s i o n _ {j}\right) = 0 \tag {52}
$$

where  $i$  ranges from 1 to 30, resulting in 30 sets of moment equilibrium equations.

To ensure reduced wear on the continuum robot body and thereby extend the robot's lifespan, it is necessary to minimize the maximum constrained cable tension:

$$
\min  _ {T e n s i o n} \left\{\max  _ {i = 1, \dots , 1 2} \{T e n s i o n _ {j} \} \right\} \tag {53}
$$

Combining Equations (52) and (53), the 12 sets of driving cable tensions  $Tension_{j}$  based on the minimization of the maximum constrained cable tension can be obtained using the Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO) algorithm [33]. This completes the collection of simulation data.

Using the Monte Carlo method, a total of 100,000 data sets were generated. Fig. 9 shows the multi

In this paper, a multilayer perceptron (MLP) neural network is used to train the mapping function  $c(\cdot)$  between the 3D Cartesian coordinates of the robot's end point and the joint angles and cable tensions. Input layer: contains the input data dimensions (3D Cartesian coordinates); First hidden layer: 128 neurons; Second hidden layer: 64 neurons; Third hidden layer: 32 neurons; Output layer: 42 neurons (30 joint angles and 12 cable tensions).

The choice of three hidden layers and the corresponding number of neurons is based on the following considerations: (a) Multiple hidden layers can extract and refine complex nonlinear features. (b) The dataset of 100,000 samples is sufficiently large to

support a complex model without easily overfitting. (c) A network with three hidden layers is feasible in terms of computational resources and training time. (d) The high-dimensional mapping between inputs and outputs requires a multilayer neural network to capture nonlinear relationships.

Through this design, a balance can be achieved between model complexity and computational efficiency, resulting in improved predictive performance.

# 5. Path Following Simulation Experiment Verification and Discussion

In the path following simulation experiment of this paper, the theoretical path to be followed is set as:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} X _ {\text {t h e o r e t i c a l}} = 2 0 0 \cdot \sin (\theta) \\ Y _ {\text {t h e o r e t i c a l}} = 2 0 0 \cdot \sin (\theta) \\ Z _ {\text {t h e o r e t i c a l}} = 3 0 0 \cdot \cos (\theta) \end{array} \right. \tag {54}
$$

The theoretical curve and the actual end-effector following curve from the simulation experiment are plotted for comparison, as shown in Fig. 10.

![image](https://cdn-mineru.openxlab.org.cn/result/2026-01-23/d6fe27db-16a9-4201-8960-3103eae29c5c/7731e3ad4b5bb771b926927f049fcf46abddbf71f89119da8a9e5f91a830c11e.jpg)



Fig. 10 Comparison of theoretical curve and actual end-effector following curve


is divided into multiple waypoints. The robot sequentially moves its end-effector to each waypoint by calculating the necessary joint angles and cable tensions. By increasing the number of waypoints, the path can be made smoother, reducing the changes in cable lengths and configurations between adjacent points. While our current implementation achieves acceptable smoothness, we acknowledge that optimizing the number of waypoints is crucial for improving path accuracy and efficiency. Future work will focus on studying the effect of waypoint density on path following performance and developing methods to enhance trajectory smoothness.

The position errors of the end-effector following curve in the X, Y, and Z axes during the path following simulation experiment are calculated as:

$$
\left\{ \begin{array}{l} E r r o r _ {X} = X _ {\text {t h e o r e t i c a l}} - X _ {\text {e x p e r i m e n t}} \\ E r r o r _ {Y} = Y _ {\text {t h e o r e t i c a l}} - Y _ {\text {e x p e r i m e n t}} \\ E r r o r _ {Z} = Z _ {\text {t h e o r e t i c a l}} - Z _ {\text {e x p e r i m e n t}} \end{array} \right. \tag {55}
$$

In the three-dimensional Cartesian coordinate system, the comprehensive error of the continuum robot's endpoint in the simulation experiment is:

$$
E r r o r _ {\text {c o m p r e h e n s i v e}} = \sqrt {\operatorname {E r r o r} _ {X} ^ {2} + \operatorname {E r r o r} _ {Y} ^ {2} + \operatorname {E r r o r} _ {Z} ^ {2}} \tag {56}
$$

As shown in Fig. 11, the error curve of the continuum robot's endpoint in the simulation experiment is illustrated. It can be seen that the maximum comprehensive error of the continuum robot's endpoint is  $9.37\mathrm{mm}$ .

Compared to control methods based solely on analytical models or data-driven approaches, it demonstrates significant advantages.

To provide perspective, our continuum robot is  $1350\mathrm{mm}$  long and weighs  $3\mathrm{kg}$ . Despite its size and weight, it achieves an absolute positioning error of  $9\mathrm{mm}$ , corresponding to a relative error of approximately  $0.67\%$ . In contrast, other continuum robots, typically less than  $250\mathrm{mm}$  in length, report higher relative errors. For instance, a  $54\mathrm{mm}$  robot had an error of  $1.14\mathrm{mm}$  ( $2.11\%$  relative error) $^{27,34}$ , a  $220\mathrm{mm}$  robot had an error of  $15.5\mathrm{mm}$  ( $7.05\%$ ) $^{28}$ , and a  $227\mathrm{mm}$  robot had an error of  $18.8\mathrm{mm}$  ( $8.29\%$ ) $^{25}$ . These comparisons show that our robot, although significantly larger and heavier, achieves a lower relative positioning error. This demonstrates the effectiveness of our control approach in handling large-scale, heavy continuum robots.

Our method achieves a computation time of 0.72 milliseconds per sample, significantly faster than the computation times reported in the literature for numerical optimization methods, which can be on the order of hundreds of milliseconds per sample. Real-time control performance remains crucial, even when the robot operates in quasi-static, low-speed scenarios. In practical control systems, master-slave control schemes are commonly employed, where the slave robot must respond promptly to the master's commands. If the control model's computations are not performed in real-time, delays can introduce uncoordinated or jerky movements, degrading the smoothness of the control. Therefore, ensuring real-time computation of the control model is necessary to maintain smooth and precise control, even at low movement speeds.

Although utilizing simulation data reduces the risk of damaging the robot during data collection, we recognize that there is a gap between the simulation model and the real robot, which can affect control accuracy. In future work, we plan to collect a limited amount of real-world data from the physical robot and apply transfer learning techniques to fine-tune our neural network models. By doing so, we aim to bridge the gap between simulation and reality, reducing errors and enhancing the robustness and effectiveness of the control methods in practical applications.

# 6. Conclusions

This paper addresses the path-following problem of cable-driven continuum robots with significant self-weight, high structural coupling, and large driving forces, particularly for inspecting and clearing structures within the confined and complex internal spaces of aircraft components. A motion control method that combines analytical models with machine learning is proposed. The main research findings are as follows:

(1) The path-following challenges of cable-driven continuum robots in confined, complex internal spaces of aircraft components, exacerbated by significant self-weight, high structural coupling, and large driving forces, are identified and analyzed.

(2) A combined approach integrating a piecewise non-uniform curvature kinematic model with quasi-static dynamic equations is proposed to address these challenges.

(3) By integrating analytical models with machine learning, the approach mitigates the issue of robot damage caused by the extensive data collection required in purely data-driven methods.

(4) Simulation experiments using a virtual prototype demonstrate significant improvements in control accuracy, reduced robot body damage, and enhanced real-time performance.

(54) Simulation results show a maximum endpoint error of  $9.37\mathrm{mm}$  and an average inference time of 0.72 ms.

For future work, we intend to conduct experiments with the physical continuum robot to validate the proposed control strategy in real-world scenarios. This will help to further demonstrate the practical applicability and robustness of our approach in actual applications.
